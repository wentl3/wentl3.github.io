Wired or wireless, fiber or copper?

distance / bandwidth / fiber / copper

Short answer

Pick the medium by the constraint that binds, because fiber and copper cover each other's weak side: copper for the short run, fiber beyond it.

Before the network jargon, start with something closer to daily life: how rail carries communication.

Start with the constraint that binds

A link has four needs that pull against each other: distance, bandwidth, delay and cost. The one that binds is the one that decides the medium.

Rail shows it. Rail networks did more than spread culture. They also pushed the distance and bandwidth that communication could use. The same split shows up in networks.

No medium wins all four. So this part sorts media by the binding constraint, not by the cable.

Wireless: when distance binds

Wireless carries the signal without a laid line. When the needed distance grows, its low build cost speeds up long-distance rollout. The article's example is a vendor's product shown under a traffic-management heading.

The limit appears at the track itself. Along the rail track the need is bandwidth, not distance, so the choice moves to wired links.

Wired: when bandwidth binds

Wired means fiber or copper cable. Along the rail track, wired links remove the bandwidth bottleneck and keep parallel signals from disturbing one another.

Wired links still pay for their bandwidth with signal delay and the high cost of laying the line.

So a wired link is the right answer only when bandwidth, not reach or rollout speed, is what binds.

Fiber and copper split the wired job

Fiber and copper complement each other the way wired and wireless do. The article splits wired use into two kinds. Fiber networks serve hubs that need long distance. Electrical-signal links serve devices such as network monitors, which are easy to connect with an electrical signal.

The split is not a ranking. It is a division of work, and each side is weak where the other is strong.

Fiber: light over long reach

Fiber carries light. Near-total internal reflection keeps signal attenuation and distortion low, so it reaches farther. An SFP optical transceiver is the article's example.

Standards put a number on "farther", and the number depends on the fiber type: a long-reach single-mode link and a short-reach multimode link do not reach the same distance. The numbers are not shown yet; see the last section.

The trade-off: the reach you get depends on the optic and the fiber you pair, not on the word "fiber".

Copper: electrical signal over short reach

Copper carries an electrical signal. Conductor impedance and signal attenuation limit how far it goes. The everyday example is Cat6 Ethernet cable with an RJ45 plug.

Structured-cabling practice fixes a channel length made of a permanent link plus patch cords, and the Ethernet standard for ten-gigabit copper allows a longer run on a higher cable category than on a lower one. The lengths are not shown yet; see the last section.

The trade-off: past that length copper gives way to fiber. That line is where the claim above flips.
