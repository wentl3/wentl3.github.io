Which cable fits a data center link, and what heat comes with it?

DAC / AEC / AOC / NIC

Short answer

In a data center, reach picks the cable class, and every class swaps one thermal problem for another: thick copper blocks airflow, while thin active cables and optics make heat of their own.

This part follows one packet through a data center and asks what each cable on the path does to the heat budget.

How a packet moves through a data center

  • A packet from a user's software goes from the carrier's base station to the data center.
  • It travels over optical fiber to a network switch, then over copper to the servers that compute.
  • After computing, the server sends the packet back through the network interface card (NIC) to the switch and the data center backbone, and on to the user.
  • Fiber to the switch and copper to the server is the economic model that spreads the cost of building a data center.

What each class costs in heat

  • DAC. The thick gauge makes cooling at the switch cluster hard and makes rack exhaust harder.
  • AEC. The chip cleans up the signal, so the wire can be thinner. It does not block airflow and is easier to route.
  • AOC. A thin fiber cable eases switch exhaust and raises cable density. The extra energy conversion adds waste heat that needs thermal design.
  • Coherent optics. They add a temperature-control element, and the heavier processor load means more energy density to handle.

The limit of the claim

In a compact data center, heat limits performance and is also one factor in PUE.

The thin cables are not a free fix: an AOC adds waste heat from energy conversion, and coherent optics add temperature-control parts and a heavier processor load, so both need a thermal design.

A pluggable cable can look like a USB lead and still need a thermal design.

The NIC end of the link

  • Data center NICs come in several form factors.
  • The heat sink on the IO side makes the contrast between transfer speed and thermal design easy to see.

Where this leaves the series

When the modules are compared, cost and physical operating mode come first and thermal design follows. High-speed IO is the infrastructure of future communication and also one indicator of data center power performance.
