Part 1 of 5

Copy the recipe once, then repeat runs

From fresh graduate to newbie thermal engineer

Short answer

As a newbie, become a sponge soaking up knowledge and follow the seniors’ “best recipe” at least once.

The fastest way from a school project into industry thermal simulation and validation is to become a sponge soaking up knowledge and follow the seniors’ “best recipe” at least once, then let repeat runs train the hands and the eyes. These tips were first written as a newbie, about the change from a contract engineer role to a full-time thermal engineer.

Having a senior engineer supervise the work from the first day was a grateful opportunity; even in a small team making a big impact, some things were too complex for a junior to digest quickly. What I realized most as a newbie is becoming a sponge soaking up knowledge and following the seniors’ “best recipe” at least once. Following others’ styles is uncomfortable, but it is the fastest way to grow a mindset, and there is usually a surprising and meaningful reward at the end.

Five tips from fresh blood to newbie (2022)

  1. Understand tools in hand and maximize advantages. Flotherm stands out in electronics cooling, yet its Cartesian grid and immersed boundary are usually the first disaster for users raised on classic points, curves and surfaces tools. Stepping out of the comfort zone is the skill you carry through a career, because no one knows which tool comes next.
  2. Simulation model as simple as possible but no simpler. Keep the model representative of the real product, not a mockup on paper. An oversimplified flow path gives a misleading flow pattern, which skews the heatsink evaluation and the failure risk; in a stacked high-speed IO connector, keep the exact vents for distribution and simplify the rest of the connector only as far as the flow pattern allows. There are many ways to simplify, but the thermal engineer is responsible for the quality of the output: the distribution matters, not only the impedance.
  3. Mesh control by levels but no total numbers. Unlike a school project with a single pure geometry, a rack chassis spreads components everywhere, and few projects leave time for a mesh independence study. Control the mesh by levels ordered by path importance; the minimum cell size method in Flotherm is flexible, while localized constraints add computational cost and crash risk. Copying a converged setup is a good start that may not work in the next case; a robust mesh mindset is what keeps the output consistent as the assembly grows.
  4. Experiment calibration from gageR&R to accuracy. Run gage R&R first: repeat the same setup across people and runs, and the spread is the noise any design difference has to beat. There is not much standard for running an experiment in a “good” way aligned with the end-user application, and the first choice, fixed flow or a fixed pressure source, decides what a result can be compared with; the next section works it through.
  5. Match validation purpose with feasible setup/assumption. Reproduce the end-user application in the wind tunnel as far as the limits allow: the fixture, the front bezel, the inlet gap and airflow direction, and the heater profile. Customers usually share little about their systems, so cross-functional work with the copper and optical product teams is what lets you make reasonable assumptions and bring the customer an architecture-level evaluation instead of separate pieces of information.

The original post closed on these as three simulation and two validation mindsets: team cultures differ, but the business target is the same, and the hope was that each reader finds a navigator at sea.

Fixed flow or fixed pressure: decide what the test compares

Think of the wind tunnel as plumbing. A fixed-flow test is a metered pump: it pushes the same litres per minute through every pipe, so each pipe is judged only on what it does with that water. A fixed-pressure test is a water tower: every pipe sees the same head, and a narrow pipe simply gets less water. A heatsink with denser fins is the narrow pipe.

The air temperature rise through a heatsink is ΔT = P / (ρ · cp · V̇), which for sea-level air works out to ΔT [°C] ≈ 1.76 × P [W] / flow [CFM]. Fixed flow, as in the original setup at 3/4/5 CFM, holds that cooling capacity constant: a 10 W module warms the air by 1.76 × 10 / 3 ≈ 5.9 °C at 3 CFM and 1.76 × 10 / 5 ≈ 3.5 °C at 5 CFM, whatever the fins look like, so the engineer can focus on the quality of the design. Under a fixed pressure source, as in the original -1.0/-1.5/-2.0 inAq settings, a heatsink with denser fins adds impedance, gets less air, and its air rise climbs, while one with sparse fins keeps more flow; the fin area added can be eaten by the air lost, which is why fixed-pressure results are hard to cross-compare but good for checking the system operating point.

Looking back in 2026

Taking on low-level, repeat experiments by hand turned out to be a better way in, and it even left time to reflect and observe. Repeat work is not wasted work: each repeat run calibrates the hands and the eyes against the result. The reason is the same arithmetic as tip four: repeat runs of one heatsink at the same flow show how far a reading wanders on its own, and only a design change larger than that wander means anything. Whoever has done the repeat runs knows that spread by heart; whoever skipped them ranks designs on noise.

The industry uses paid tools, but a newcomer can start from hands-on work that is online and free. A newcomer can even pair that free work with AI and MCP (Model Context Protocol) to build something that works. At the time, [Onshape](https://www.onshape.com) for online CAD and [SimScale](https://www.simscale.com) for online CAE were free and ran on cloud computing, while coding grew fast on open resources and CAD and CAE were rarely mentioned; their plan terms may have changed since. A free tool is where the repeat runs can begin.

Three things from the second year

TopicWhat happenedFor a newcomer
DebuggingA high-end GPU platform had trouble in the field, with two things stacked together. A GPU next to the PCIe switch overheated because the thermal interface material (TIM) had been reused and made poor contact, and replacing the cooling module brought the margin back. Another GPU dropped off the bus, and only after the TIM was replaced did it show that the TIM's electrical conductivity caused the failure. Another time, on memory, telemetry read normal while a surface thermocouple showed the PMIC overheating; a three-way comparison of telemetry, thermocouple and simulation settled it.The first step in debugging is to suspect the measurement itself and cross-check with at least two methods. Once the root cause is found, report the risk to whoever has the authority to decide, such as the ODM, and let them decide on the redesign.
VOCA customer and the ODM could not agree on the boundary for inlet water temperature. I ran a fixed load to a 1-hour steady state, stepped the water temperature level by level, and repeated the measurements to confirm reproducibility. In the end the acceptance condition became a threshold on the thermal margin of the surrounding components, signed by the ODM.What a customer states as a requirement is often just a number. VOC means asking for the boundary scenario behind it, then turning that into a condition that can be measured and signed.
Limit samplesAt a connector company I worked for before, on heat-pipe cooling modules from POC to pre-PVT, two suppliers with two lots each came in with about half out of spec; with outliers removed the data fell in a normal distribution, centered about 1σ from the spec. I chose the cross-section locations from the module's integration breakpoints and the critical heat-source areas, and the root cause was a fixture thermal-expansion mismatch plus too fast a temperature ramp. I proposed a one-sided upper limit with the process pulled to 3σ inside the spec; the judgment document went to a dedicated quality inspector, who owns day-to-day release.A limit sample turns “how bad is still acceptable” into a line you can measure. Thermal design sets the criteria and the direction of the root cause; execution and release belong to quality inspection and the suppliers.

Starting work was confusing: many documents on the internet, and little industry knowledge to sort them with, from the mechanical mating datum to the thermal simulation setup. Two overview pieces were written as a guide for mechanical and thermal engineers, one on where high-speed IO sits in servers and switches and one breaking down the module specifications; they now live in the high-speed IO series. Knowing what thermal design work is done in a component, and why it matters in the system, is an enjoyable place for interest to start.
