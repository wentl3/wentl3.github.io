From one part to the whole system

Path into engineering

Short answer

Each step traded a familiar setting for a wider one, and the career kept the same pattern, from one cooling part to a whole node system.

I didn't know why a port needed cooling; now I validate the hardware of a whole node. The road between those two points widened one ring at a time: from passive cooling cage parts, to active components across departments, to node-level hardware system validation across hardware companies, all driven by product specifications. Every school step before it followed the same rule, trading a familiar setting for a wider one.

Picture a server rack opened from the inside out. The innermost box is one cage around one port; around it sits the module that makes the heat; around that, the whole node the module plugs into. Each box contains the last one, so each step outward keeps the old problem and adds every neighbour it now touches.

Hands first

In vocational school I wanted a practical trade rather than more basic academic subjects, and I learned a great deal from hands-on work and from drilling the fundamentals before taking the leap, which taught something different from sitting in a classroom holding a pen. That gap between workshop and classroom is the line between the two education systems, an argument that has run for years with little change to the system. Crossing it as a vocational high-school student, into a general university rather than a technical one, brought a culture shock in how classes worked and in what being an engineer meant. The two tracks start from opposite ends. The vocational track starts from the hands, with a trade and fundamentals drilled until they hold; the general university starts from the basic subjects, taught at a desk with a pen. A student who crosses from one to the other brings the hands along but arrives owing the basics, and paying off that debt is where the next ring begins.

Survive first

My grades were good enough to squeeze into the single transfer seat at a general university, which made me happy but put me under more pressure. My classmates no longer came from one local school district but from across the country, the best of their year, and I had to survive among them.

Survive first; learning comes after. Reading textbooks in English, absorbing the material, working through past exam papers, building contacts and using the student association's resources were all things I had to survive. I also had to jump from a junior-high foundation to university work and fill in all of high-school physics and chemistry, which was far from easy. Why did it feel like survival rather than study? The school was built for depth, and depth assumes the basics are already in place. National Chiao Tung University is a top research university set close to Hsinchu Science Park, the "Silicon Valley" of Taiwan, and it is known for close collaboration between academia and industry. Its colleges span engineering, electrical engineering, semiconductor technology, computer science and photonics among others; it offers interdisciplinary minors, hands-on mini-courses and shared maker spaces, and its postgraduates have national research centres and laboratories on their doorstep. Today it has merged with National Yang-Ming University to become National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University. National Chiao Tung University was rich in research but boring as a campus, and students stayed in the dorm without knowing why they were studying. A campus like that rewards the student who arrives with the basics; the one who arrives without them has to survive first.

Pressure becomes manageable once it is written down as a list of things to survive. That was the first ring outward: a familiar workshop traded for a nationwide classroom, paid for in survival.

Knowing why

Then came the chance to apply for an exchange at SUTD, and before the scholarship came through I worried, like an impostor, that I was not good enough to be there. Once I had survived the first stretch, I found that my classmates knew why they had come to university and carried real ambitions.

Knowing why you learn gives the learning a direction. The university gave every student a common foundation regardless of department, along with a real sense of academic freedom, and without knowing why they were there, nobody would carry the pressure of high tuition. The same subjects came with less chalkboard writing, more efficient teaching of theory, more hands-on courses and calculation drills, and teaching assistants and group work so that students learned from each other. Even the regular daytime hours included hands-on work split among teaching assistants, while professors focused on clear explanation and designed courses around chosen topics.

Set side by side, the two campuses did not differ for lack of resources; the research university at home had plenty. They differed in where the reason to learn lived. SUTD was set up to nurture technically grounded leaders and innovators, and its curriculum was first built with MIT, whose faculty drew up the blueprint and co-taught subjects alongside SUTD faculty; a school that says from the start whom it means to train can write that reason into the timetable. At home it was left to each student alone in the dorm, while in Singapore it was built into the daytime timetable, with teaching assistants running the hands-on work. The classroom furthest from home turned out to feel the most like the vocational workshop, and that pointed to the next step. I chose not to pursue a master's degree and went into industry for a different kind of classroom, just as in vocational school: I would rather learn by doing.

From parts to systems

The work started as a contract mechanical engineer and became a full-time thermal design role. At a connector company the job was thermal design for high-speed optical transceivers, bringing thermal and mechanical limits into industry specification discussions. Next came a liquid cooling company: leading its Asia-Pacific lab, building a high-temperature liquid cooling lab from nothing, managing several suppliers, and setting the facility water loop and acceptance criteria. At every ring the specification comes first: the system or switch side fixes the form factor and the target power, with some margin, and from that you can estimate the use case and data rate, pick the pluggable module and its layout, and feed the proposal and the thermal simulation. The habit a newcomer can borrow first comes from field debugging: once, telemetry said a memory part was normal while a surface thermocouple said its power IC was overheating, and only a three-way check of telemetry, thermocouple and simulation settled it; so before trusting a reading, suspect the measurement itself, cross-check it by at least two methods, and report the risk to whoever owns the decision. Around those companies sits the widest ring, the place the hardware comes from. Taiwan dominates the foundry market, the outsourcing of semiconductor manufacturing, and its contract manufacturers count major technology firms among their clients; when a global chip shortage forced carmakers to halt production, that role came under the spotlight.

Taiwan is a small island with patient people and a leading place in semiconductors. When the conflict in Ukraine had just begun and hardly anyone was paying attention to Taiwan, I used the story of a small country standing up to a large one to show, through industry and through differences in history and culture, that Chinese culture is not only China. Today the Taiwanese supply chain is famous while AI hardware is in the spotlight, yet explaining Taiwan's context and democratic values matters too, for its international image, for a personal brand, for soft power, and for a warm, human sense of history and literature alongside a career in technical writing.

For those who come after: the classroom is not the only place to learn, and learning by doing is a path of its own. The next classroom of practice is one ring further out in each series: high-speed ports and their thermal design, the working methods in the toolkit, and liquid cooling at rack scale.
