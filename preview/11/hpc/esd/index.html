Stage 2 of 6 · Build the lab

Static control in an HPC lab is one common ground, and humidity decides how hard it works

ESD / grounding / EPA

Short answer

Establish an equipotential system: connect all conductive and dissipative materials to a common ground.

In a dense HPC lab, static control is not only about equipment stability: it also protects product quality and operational safety. The answer is one equipotential system: connect all conductive and dissipative materials to a common ground, so no potential difference is left to discharge across.

Think of the spark from a doorknob on a dry winter day. The same hand on the same door gives nothing on a humid summer day: what changed is the water in the air, which leaves a thin film on surfaces that lets charge leak away before it can jump.

Where charge comes from, and three principles

Most charge comes from friction: two unlike materials touch and separate, and electrons move from one to the other, as rabbit fur charges positive and Teflon negative. In a lab that means handling parts, walking on carpet and fluid flowing fast through pipes, and a larger contact area or a faster separation makes more charge; induction, ion bombardment, high-pressure gas and water or steam spray charge things too, and dry air lets the charge stay. A sensitive semiconductor part can be damaged permanently, and a spark can ignite vapors. The control follows three principles: suppress the charge at its source, dissipate what builds up through the common ground, and neutralize what cannot be grounded.

Those sources turn into buying and layout rules. Suppress at the source by choosing materials low in the triboelectric series, or with anti-static additives or coatings. Keep the induction sources out of reach: plastic packaging, paper cups and personal belongings stay outside the area, and an insulator whose field exceeds 2 kV per inch keeps at least 30 cm from sensitive devices or gets an ion blower. Buying by material also means sorting by resistance. Conductive material, under 10 kΩ, sends charge to ground fast but does little to limit a sudden large current. Dissipative material, from there up to 100 GΩ, lets charge leave at a controlled rate, which is why benches, floors and packaging are dissipative; a dissipative floor holds its surface resistance between 100 kΩ and 1 GΩ, low enough to give the discharge a path and high enough to soften it. Insulating material, above that, cannot be drained through the ground at all, so it is labeled and kept outside the protected area or neutralized with ionizers.

Setting up the area is a shopping list

Whoever sets up a lab meets ESD control as a purchase list and a receiving routine: what has to arrive certified, what each person wears, and what gets checked every day. The list draws a boundary, the Electrostatic Protection Area (EPA) around sensitive items such as IC components and PCB modules, inside which every tool, container and operator is tied to the common ground from receipt through assembly, testing and packaging. Follow the path a part takes, from the box it arrives in, through the hands that carry it, to the bench and the cart it lands on.

The metal chassis and the work surface are bonded straight to the ground by welding or clamping, and the strap, garment and shoes tie the person to the same ground, so body and equipment sit at the same potential. Same potential is not the same charge: a larger object has more capacitance and stores more charge at a lower voltage, so a person and a board on one ground carry different amounts of charge. What the ground removes is the potential difference between them, and that difference is what drives a discharge.

Humidity: absolute and relative

Asia is hotter and more humid: the absolute water content differs, and the relative humidity shifts with it. The same relative humidity stands for a different amount of water in a different climate. Relative humidity is a ratio: the water in the air divided by the most the air could hold at its temperature. Freezing winter air can hold at most about 4.85 g of water per cubic metre and room-temperature air about 17.3 g, so winter air warmed indoors with no water added sits under 30% relative humidity, where the moisture film on surfaces disappears and air becomes a better insulator. Air from a hot, humid day carries more water than room-temperature air can hold, so cooling it indoors pushes relative humidity up rather than down. So a range quoted without its temperature says little: the same water in the air reads as a higher relative humidity when the air is a few degrees cooler, which is why the original's two versions, 40% to 60% in English and 60% to 70% in Chinese, cannot be compared as they stand. So write a humidity spec with its temperature, or as water content, and check which way the local climate pushes relative humidity before choosing to humidify or dry the air.

Coolant charges itself

A liquid-cooled rack adds a charge source that humidity cannot touch. A coolant or oil below 100 pS/m in conductivity charges by friction against the pipe wall as it flows fast, and can build more than 1 mJ in a short time, enough to ignite vapor or mist if it discharges nearby. So the control is built into the loop at design time: pipes and tanks are metal or conductive composite with every joint on the common ground, flow ramps up and down gradually rather than starting at full speed, and the system waits a dwell time before startup, set from the liquid's dielectric constant and conductivity, so the charge already in the liquid relaxes before the first valve opens.
