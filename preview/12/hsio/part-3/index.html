Part 3 of 5

Choose a data center cable by reach first, then budget the heat that reach adds

DAC / AEC / AOC / NIC

Short answer

Choose by reach first, then by heat: every class that reaches further adds an active part, and that part's watts go into the budget on the day the project opens.

Press start on a phone's navigation, and the route comes back within moments. How does the data get sent, computed and returned that fast? The previous part covered connector forms and fiber and copper networks; this one follows that question into the data centers of the network cloud, where computing is spread out while messages are still sent and received centrally.

A user's packet goes from the carrier's base station to the data center of the software service. Fiber carries it to the network switches, and copper hands it to the server cluster that computes. When the computing is done, the network interface card (NIC) sends the packet back to the switch and the data center backbone, and on to the user; the split keeps the cost of building a data center spread out.

Cable classes: DAC / AEC / AOC / coherent

Across the data center, high-speed IO splits into three pluggable classes by reach, and the list runs to four: DAC, AEC, AOC and coherent optics. In a compact data center, cooling limits performance and is also one factor in PUE, so each class carries a heat cost as well as a reach.

So choose a data center cable by reach first, then budget the heat that reach adds. Reach is where the choice starts, not where it ends: each step down the table buys distance by adding one more part that draws power, a signal chip, a conversion to light, a temperature controller and heavier computation. Read the table that way, top to bottom, and the last column is the layer of heat each step stacks on the one before; that stack is what the wattage and margin fixed at project start have to cover.

2026 update

The limit of a copper cable has to be read together with rate and distance: as rates rose, copper reach shrank from 15 m in the early days to 2 m now, after the rate upgrades.

Why it gets hot

Copper behaves like a voice down a hallway: the faster you talk, the shorter the hall in which the words still arrive clear. That is why copper reach shrinks each time the rate climbs. An AEC posts a listener halfway who repeats every word, and an AOC swaps the voice for a flashlight; both reach further, and both pay for it in power that turns into heat: the DAC is plain copper, the AEC adds a chip, the AOC adds the conversion to light, and coherent optics add temperature control and heavier computation. That is why a cable that looks like a USB lead still gets a heat sink over its port on a data center NIC.

When a system maker or the switch side opens a project, it specifies the form factor and the target wattage up front, with a safety margin built in. The wattage and its margin come first; the modules are chosen to fit inside them.

The wattage and the margin still let the team estimate the application scenario and the transfer speed, choose which pluggable modules to pair and how to distribute them, and hand that to the proposal and the thermal simulation. The margin matters most when the rate climbs: copper reach shrinks with each rate upgrade, so a link that once fit a DAC may now need a chip or light, and the watts step up with it. Plan each rate upgrade as a cable-class review: list which runs fall past the new copper reach, and add their chips' watts to the margin before the modules are picked.

The heat problem of a cable is half decided on the day the project opens.

At every layer, reliable and energy-saving high-speed IO is required, and it is one indicator of how a data center performs on power.
