Part 4 of 5

IO sits in two places in an Open Compute Project system, and the rear NIC in hot air is the harder one to cool

server NIC / switch front panel / hot aisle / cold aisle

Short answer

In general, the hot aisle is much more difficult in thermal design.

IO sits in two places in an Open Compute Project system, the network interface card (NIC) at the rear of a server and the front panel of a switch, and the rear card is the harder one to cool because it breathes air the CPU has already heated.

Two intake cases are evaluated: a cold aisle, free ambient air from the data center, and a hot aisle, air already heated by the parts upstream. The hot-aisle case is harder not only for the warmer inlet: by the time the air reaches the rear card it has spread across the whole box instead of being aimed at the IO, which is why a typical IO heatsink adds side and rear fins to guide the air.

Think of the two places as seats on a bus whose only open windows are at the front. The switch front panel sits by the window and gets the coolest air, but the ASIC behind it needs most of that breeze, so the panel cannot block it. The server NIC sits in the back row and breathes air that the passengers in front, the CPU and memory, have already warmed. That back-row seat costs the card directly, because the temperature budget works by subtraction. With the conduction temperature difference from chip to air fixed by the heat sink, every degree the air gains upstream is a degree taken from the chip's margin. As an example, if the CPU and memory warm the air by 10 °C before it reaches the rear card, the NIC chip runs 10 °C hotter with the same heat sink, while the front panel never sees that preheat.

Why it gets hot

A switch front panel is more likely to sit in the cold aisle, because a dense panel is where modules are plugged and serviced, but it has to make up for the smaller share of local airflow it gets, may not even know in advance whether the fans will still deliver enough pressure at the panel once the whole system is running, and cannot carry so much impedance that the ASIC behind it is short of air. The bottleneck is set by where a part sits in the airflow, not by which part runs hottest.

A cage is built around the outside shape, the form factor, and the cooling concept centers on that passive part; whether it will hold an optical module or an external laser source is not known. So the association's specification gives a suggested cooling capacity in watts, to read together with different airflow, pressure and system impedance conditions. Under the panel the height is limited and the space to the left and right cannot be used, and when the stacked cage sits on one side, the inner layer has less open space and fewer channels for air. In a stacked cage, the inner heatsink is limited by the connector and the EMI cage opening, the extra impedance lets air bypass the front bezel into the system, and so the bottom port, a design each connector maker keeps in-house, draws more attention. Because switch applications hold the larger market share, connector companies keep developing products for more thermal channeling in exactly these ganged and stacked cages. That is how position in the airflow, more than heat alone, becomes the bottleneck. Draw how the air moves first, then talk about the module; the bottleneck shows up along that path.

The height is not settled

When the original article ran, the specification defined a few form factors at two card heights, and taller card proposals were in discussion, because the growing heat load, such as active electrical cables at rack reach, pushes on both the thermal design and the height limit. The height difference lets the card take IO in different form factors, which opens compatibility and also eases cooling on the larger OSFP compared with the dense QSFP and QSFP-DD. The height is still not settled: the specification bounds a range rather than fixing one, and the yearly member submissions push the thermal limit in lower but denser NIC cards.

How a proposal persuades

Correlated thermal models let a simulation show the flow rate a design needs under the two intake conditions. A NIC is watched at two components: the IO connector, which the specification treats as having no thermal issue at the power it considers, and the ASIC, whose maximum TDP is the critical outcome, evaluated against fan flow rate, inlet temperature, and cold or hot aisle. For a customer to accept a proposal, meeting the required wattage and the boundary cases for airflow direction and flow rate is only the baseline; persuading further takes a more integrated simulation with the card's local placement in the system, or verification at different altitudes and with different module applications, because a complete, comprehensive account always helps. When I proposed a cooling design to a customer, I could say how many watts it would solve, but the customer asked where that wattage came from: was the reference an AOC, an AEC or a transceiver, how long would it hold, to which generation, at what bandwidth and rate. So the report could not be a one-sided thermal number.

2026: Liquid cooling is already in use, and an external laser source (ELS) proof of concept has also appeared in applications.
