Stage 4 of 6 · Deploy

Speed, not energy saving, now decides how liquid cooling is deployed

history / fluid / maintenance / speed

Short answer

Speed in production, deployment and maintenance now decides the choice, which favors direct liquid cooling, while immersion keeps the simpler service for individual nodes within a rack.

Speed now decides how liquid cooling goes into a data center, and that favors direct liquid cooling, though immersion keeps an edge in service. Immersion cooling maintenance can be more straightforward than DLC, especially for individual nodes within a rack. DLC brings the coolant to the components through cold plates: maintenance is repeatable, with regular checks for leaks or corrosion in plates and pipes, coolant level and top-up, filter changes and coolant quality, but taking apart one IT component tied into a complex cold-plate system is harder. Immersion submerges the components fully, and its upkeep resembles air cooling plus HVAC checks: fluid level, regular fluid-quality analysis, and inspecting the tanks for leaks or damage.

DLC is like the water jacket of a car engine: coolant runs in sealed lines to the hot parts, the rest stays dry, and replacing one part means opening a line. Immersion is like a deep fryer: everything sits in the oil, a part comes out by lifting the basket, and the oil itself becomes the thing to look after.

Fluid and maintenance

Insulating oil cooled high-voltage transformers long before computers; immersion came back to high-performance computing as processing power and heat density rose. Single-phase is the common form, where the liquid stays liquid and carries heat by convection; two-phase lets the liquid boil around hot parts and moves heat through vapor and condensation, at the cost of a more complex design. Higher upfront cost, maintenance complexity and scalability in large data centers are the known challenges, and implementing liquid cooling takes specialized knowledge, which is why open computing consortia and hardware makers work together on specifications, product development and warranty support. Meanwhile direct liquid cooling (DLC) remains a primary solution for HPC and data centers, coexisting with existing infrastructure. The fluids themselves keep moving: newer coolants raise thermal conductivity and cut flow losses while answering dielectric breakdown voltage, biodegradability and environmental impact, and ongoing research looks for dielectric fluids compatible with a wider range of components, covering material compatibility, aging, and validating bandwidth and latency.

Fluid is chosen for performance, safety and lifespan, and kept compatible with system materials to prevent leaks and corrosion. Deionized water conducts heat well and costs little but needs purity upkeep; glycol mixtures carry corrosion inhibitors; synthetic hydrocarbons conduct heat well but are flammable; synthetic esters are environmentally friendly but may conduct heat less well; fluorinated liquids perform well and do not burn, but cost more and carry environmental impact. Additives such as corrosion inhibitors, biocides and defoamers extend component life, and keeping fluid quality is the hard part of operation. Two-phase fluorinated fluids remain restricted under PFAS rules. Competition among oil and chemical suppliers lets computer makers iterate chip platforms more efficiently, but it also exposes material and chemical compatibility: picking the oil and validating it with the equipment is what brings a load online stably, spec-sheet figures of merit (FOM) help weigh it against fire protection, and a high-temperature heat-recovery room wants a more fluid oil than a low-temperature, wide-span chip-tuning room, which wants a stable composition. Handling and disposal follow regulations for the environment and worker safety; operators are trained on the specific coolant and on leak detection; pumps, fans and heat exchangers carry MTBF ratings, with DLC's extra parts on preventive schedules while immersion stays close to traditional IT maintenance without vendor lock-in; and both track temperature, conductivity, particle count and dielectric strength against clear alarm thresholds.

Speed over energy saving

I favor direct liquid cooling over single-phase immersion because the direction at chipmakers and in hardware architecture has shifted sharply, and what counts now is speed. So the deciding factor in a deployment is speed, read here as the number of steps between a delivered rack and its first workload.

The market volume and the quality validation are both in place, and overall it is good enough. What is left to compare is the steps. For a 300 kW rack both need the same facility side, including 1200 LPM of water, so the difference in speed lies in what comes after. DLC hardware follows the chipmakers' platforms and coexists with existing infrastructure, while immersion adds its own steps before the first node goes in: tanks on a floor checked for their weight, fluid supply, and training on handling and disposing of the fluid. Energy saving is no longer everyone's main consideration; speed is, in production, deployment and maintenance alike. So count the steps before counting the energy saved.

Heat capture and the white room

Speed favors DLC, but DLC has a limit of its own: the cold plates capture only part of the heat, and whatever they miss still has to leave by air. That remainder brings air cooling back, either as a rear-door heat exchanger or as a fully sealed rack, and each is another layer of maintenance and build complexity on top of the liquid loop. White-room aisles used to be kept tight because the space was precious, but as total heat doubles the existing aisles have to grow too, and nobody dares to push density per unit any further. Immersion captures a high share of the heat in the fluid, which lifts that burden off the infrastructure; it does not even need clean air. That releases the costly limits of the white room, so the load and its data can sit anywhere, including remote sites.

Water and dry coolers

Air cooling leans on evaporative water to bring cooling water near the wet-bulb temperature, which hurts in water-scarce regions and loses efficiency in hot, humid weather; DLC and immersion take heat straight from the equipment, so the plant can run at warmer cooling water and depends less on chillers and the outside climate. Air conditioning loses efficiency in heat and humidity and needs extra heating in cold climates, while liquid that takes heat straight off the equipment keeps working at a warmer room temperature. From experience, cooling takes a large share of a data center's total water use. The goal is infrastructure fully compatible with dry coolers, which cool the fluid loop with ambient air and no water.

Dry coolers in cooler, drier climates cool the fluid loop with ambient air and no water, and smaller chillers or none at all cut capital expense and energy use. How much they save is stated here from experience, not as a cited percentage.

So, back to where immersion started: when the new approaches change faster than I can keep up with, I would first focus on the simple, reliable way, as before, with the hardware submerged in insulating fluid. That is where the high-speed IO series picks the thread back up, in its part on keeping things simple and reliable.
