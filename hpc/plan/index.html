The earlier chapters followed one system from the reason it needs liquid cooling, through the lab where it is tested, into the room where it runs. The last step comes before the room is built: deciding how it will be cooled. This chapter does not pick a winner. It puts the three methods on the same inputs so the choice can be made for a specific room.

What TUE measures

Power usage effectiveness (PUE) compares the energy a facility draws with the energy delivered to its IT equipment. It hides one thing: inside each server, part of the IT energy goes to fans and power conversion instead of computing. Total-usage effectiveness (TUE) adds that part back. It multiplies the server's own overhead (ITUE) by the facility's cooling and power overhead, so a lower TUE means more of every watt drawn from the grid reaches the processors.

Every cooling method moves the same two levers: how much fan power stays inside the server, and how much work the facility does to reject the heat. The three sections below follow those two levers, then say what each method asks of the room and what is still missing.

Air cooling

Fans move all of the heat out of the server, and the room's air handling and chillers carry it away. Fan power therefore stays inside the server's own overhead, and the facility works hardest of the three. Air cooling needs no liquid in the rack and fits rooms that already run on computer room air conditioning. Its limits are density and climate: efficiency falls as rack density rises, evaporative cooling uses water, and hot, humid air makes the plant work harder. For this design point the server's power is the vendor's own estimate, and its fan power is estimated from the fan maker's datasheet.

Direct liquid cooling

Cold plates take heat straight from the processors, and fans move the rest. Fan power falls in proportion to the heat the liquid captures, and the facility can reject that part of the heat with warmer water and less chiller work. Direct liquid cooling supports higher rack densities and coexists with existing air-cooled infrastructure; its maintenance is regular and repeatable, though components tied into a cold-plate loop are harder to take apart, and the extra pumps and fittings need preventive maintenance. For this design point the captured share comes from the vendor's spec sheet, and the server's power is the average of two vendor planning tools.

Immersion

The whole server sits in a dielectric fluid, so its fans are removed and all of its heat goes to the liquid. Fan power leaves the server's overhead entirely, and the facility can reject the heat with warm water, in some climates without chillers. Immersion handles the highest densities of the three and needs no air-handling infrastructure in the rack; it asks for tanks, fluid handling and staff training, and its maintenance looks like air cooling plus fluid checks. For this design point the fan and liquid shares follow from the method itself, the server's power is the air-cooled server's estimate without its fans, and the facility model gives one value without a range.

How to read the table

Each row is one input to TUE, with the same rule for every column. Vendor figures name their document and capture date; entries that follow from how the method works are written in words; estimates come from the planning tool with their sample count and, where the model computes one, their range. The last row is the result. Read it together with the rows above it: the immersion result rests on one sample and has no computed range, so the gap between the methods is less certain than the single figures suggest.

How it is calculated

Every cell comes from the author's planning tool and the vendor documents named in it, including vendor planning tools that need a free account. Each estimate states its sample count and, where the model computes one, its range. The final TUE row is shown for all three methods together or for none.
