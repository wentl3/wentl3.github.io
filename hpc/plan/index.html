The earlier chapters followed one system from the reason it needs liquid cooling, through the lab where it is tested, into the room where it runs. The last step comes before the room is built: deciding how it will be cooled.

What TUE measures

Power usage effectiveness (PUE) compares the energy a facility draws with the energy delivered to its IT equipment. It hides one thing: inside each server, part of the IT energy goes to fans and power conversion instead of computing. Total-usage effectiveness (TUE) adds that part back. It multiplies the server's own overhead (ITUE) by the facility's cooling and power overhead, so a lower TUE means more of every watt drawn from the grid reaches the processors.

That makes TUE the fairer way to compare cooling methods. Liquid cooling changes both factors at once: it removes most of the fan power inside the server and it lets the facility reject heat with warmer water and less chiller work.

The comparison

The comparison uses one design point: an HGX cluster of thirty-two eight-GPU B200 nodes. Air cooling and direct liquid cooling are each represented by one vendor server whose power and heat-capture figures are published.

Immersion is left out on purpose. The tool's best immersion configuration depends on a reference design that has no public source, and the next one depends on inputs that are still missing. Following the rule for this site, a figure without a public source is not shown, and the rest of the chapter is published without it.

How to read the estimates

Each value is an estimate, not a vendor's own figure: no single document states a TUE for these systems. The range is the low and high result of the same calculation, and n is the number of vendor samples behind the value. Both ranges are narrow, so the gap between air and direct liquid cooling is larger than the uncertainty inside either one.
