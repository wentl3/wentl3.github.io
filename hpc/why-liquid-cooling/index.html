Stage 1 of 6 · Why

Air cannot carry a dense rack's heat, so HPC brings liquid to the source

rack density / air cooling limit / liquid cooling

Short answer

Per volume and per degree, water carries thousands of times more heat than air, so a rack planned in the hundreds of kilowatts is cooled by bringing liquid to the heat source, through cold plates or immersion.

Anyone who has stood next to a Bitcoin mining rig already knows the problem: the fans are loud, the air coming out is hot, and on a hot day the rig cools worse, because air cooling is limited by the temperature of the room it breathes. Miners met that wall early and answered it by sinking whole rigs in non-conductive liquid, which took the heat away evenly and the noise with it. HPC racks now meet the same wall at a much larger scale, and the reason sits in the air itself.

Computing grew from specialist engineering simulation into general generative models in everyday use, and as the computing load rose, managing heat in deployment and maintenance became harder; it takes real effort to tune, not just matching the numbers on a spec sheet. For the facility, the numbers are 600 A and 1200 LPM, and I take 300 kW as a single rack.

Heat flux and total power climb together

HPC needs liquid cooling because air runs out before the rack does: per cubic metre and per degree, water holds about 4,170 kJ of heat and air about 1.21 kJ, so the same heat needs about 3,450 times the volume of air. That gap decides the matter at this scale: at 300 kW in one rack and 1200 LPM of facility water, the heat leaves the rack position through liquid, and at so generous a flow the water warms by only about 3.6 °C.

The main heat sources are still the two big compute chips, but peripheral parts such as network cards are raising their power faster, and board space keeps them from growing the way compute chips do, so heat flux density and total power climb together. In the original draft, device power doubled from 700 W to 1500 W while chips demanded a lower surface temperature: total heat and total temperature go up while the surface has to stay cooler. That paradox pushed direct liquid cooling and immersion forward, through water with better heat transfer or a large volume of insulating oil.

Where air cooling approaches its limits

High-Performance Computing (HPC) uses supercomputers and computer clusters for problems too large or too slow for a single PC or even a rack of servers, combining many computers, often thousands, into nodes that process in parallel. Scale-out performance comes from configuring multiple systems as one and distributing computation to more nodes, so control and cooling between nodes become crucial. Traditional air cooling relies on coolers and Computer Room Air Conditioning (CRAC); as maximum rack density rises, its efficiency decreases, and pushing more airflow only complicates matters because energy efficiency is low. The system ends up both heat-constrained and wasteful with energy.

Air cooling is like cooling a pot of soup by running a fan across the kitchen: plenty of air moves, and little of it touches the pot. Direct liquid cooling runs a water pipe straight to the heat source, and immersion sets the whole pot in a cold bath. In air cooling the heat goes chip, heatsink, room air, CRAC; in direct liquid cooling it goes chip, cold plate, CDU, facility water. Every stage that passes through air is a stage through the weak carrier, repeated across the thousands of nodes a cluster scales out to. From experience, an air-cooled rack reaches its top quickly as density rises.

Early adopters: Bitcoin mining

I keep mining rigs here as an example: an early adopter case worth sharing. In the field, many machines designed for cold-plate liquid cooling end up running submerged in immersion fluid instead. An early adopter runs the trial and error before the rest of the industry follows: miners paid first for the tanks, the fluid maintenance and the coolant choices that data centers weigh now.

Mining rigs make a clean case because their hardware is simpler. Their ASIC chips are consistent with the motherboard, so a large aluminum extruded heatsink can dissipate the heat, while a server has a complex motherboard with multiple modules and chips whose high thermal density demands specialized independent cooling. Mining farms also have easier network management because the nodes connect in parallel. The original set the Antminer S9 Hydro and the Antminer S21 beside AI servers to show that gap: a rig is one chip type on one board, a server is many. Immersion fully submerges mining rigs in non-conductive liquid that conducts heat better than air, dissipating it rapidly and evenly, with lower noise and in some cases waste heat recovery.

Before judging a new cooling technology, look at which early adopter already runs it.

Direct liquid cooling or immersion

Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) circulates coolant directly over heat components such as CPUs and GPUs; it supports higher rack densities and improves energy efficiency. Immersive cooling submerges the hardware entirely in non-conductive liquid and removes the need for air cooling infrastructure, handling higher rack densities still, and within a single cabinet the chip and node temperature differences stay small. From experience, both carry a rack well past where air cooling stops, and immersion goes further still. Immersion also brings initial investment, maintenance of the liquid cooling system, and decisions about coolant selection and handling.

Many teams turn to immersion not for novelty but because it can take both air-cooled and water-cooled equipment: even if an air or water deployment falls short of plan or still carries a heavy air-conditioning load, the immersion tank can hold it after a simple modification, at the price of adapting to fluid maintenance. In a data center the cooling system is the main source of energy consumption while it keeps equipment at its optimal temperature; hot and cold aisle closures and natural air cooling have improved efficiency, liquid cooling in high-density configurations further reduces the thermal footprint, and liquid immersion cooling will be one of the key directions for improving data center energy efficiency. On the rack side, the next topics are the ORv3 high power rack, the ORv3 liquid cooled busbar, and signal integrity in immersion. So once a rack is past what air can carry, the question is no longer whether to use liquid but which path fits the room and the team: how often parts come out, whether facility water reaches the rack, and who will look after the fluid.
