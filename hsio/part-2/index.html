Part 2 of 5

Wireless rides on wired fiber, and at 800G the choke point moves inside the server

distance / bandwidth / fiber / copper / PCIe

Short answer

What looks wireless rides a wired link: fiber for the long run, copper for the short one. At 800G the cable outside is no longer the limit; the PCIe link back into the server is.

Communication is an everyday word now, but before network and high-speed IO jargon, start with the system closest to daily life: rail. Rail networks spread culture, and on the technical side they also pushed how far and how wide communication could reach. That technical side is still at work today. A rail line now runs its own communication network, and the questions it raised, how far, how wide, wired or wireless, run on through everyday networking and up to the network card in a server.

Rail signalling and edge communication make a good comparison for the question this part answers. What does the data actually run on, and what does networking in daily life look like? What looks wireless rides a wired link behind the scenes, and at 800G the narrowest part of that link is no longer the cable outside but the PCIe connection inside the server. Rail signalling and edge communication are common applications that people assume are all wireless, but they rely on wired high-speed fiber behind the scenes.

Wireless on the surface, wired behind the scenes

Rail pushed communication on two axes, distance and bandwidth. Wireless covers distance: as reach grows, its low build cost speeds up long-distance rollout. Along the track the need is bandwidth, not distance, and wired fiber or cable removes that bottleneck while keeping parallel signals from disturbing one another, at the price of signal delay and the high cost of laying the line. Fiber and copper then split the wired job the way wireless and wired split the whole: fiber carries light for hubs that need long distance, and copper carries an electrical signal to devices such as network monitors that are easy to connect with an electrical output, which is what everyday networking runs on.

Fiber reaches much further because total internal reflection keeps attenuation and distortion low, with an SFP optical transceiver as the example. Copper is held to short runs by conductor impedance and signal attenuation, as with Cat6 cable on an RJ45 plug.

Why it gets hot

The heat sits in the active parts: the chip, the laser source and the modulator, plus the board components that power integrity work brings into view. Knowing how they are laid out above and below one another shows where the thermal bottleneck is. The limit is set by each part's own temperature ceiling, not by its watts: a laser loses output and drifts in wavelength as it warms, so it can fail first even when the chip beside it gives off more heat, and a laser sitting above that chip receives its heat as well.

At 800G the choke point moves inside the server

Network interface cards have moved from 400G to 800G. Extending the external link through cages and pluggables and then testing it back in the system showed that internal transfer is another choke point. At 800G, internal PCIe 5 struggles to keep up, and PCIe 6 almost always arrives together with a new mainboard rather than as a separate upgrade. Each speed step outside has to be checked against the next narrower link inside, because the choke point moves rather than disappears.

The arithmetic shows how narrow that internal link is. Each PCIe Gen 5 lane runs 32 GT/s, so PCIe Gen 5 x16 moves 512 Gb/s each way before encoding overhead, well short of 800G: the choke point sits on the PCIe link, not on the cable. PCIe Gen 6 doubles the lane rate to 64 GT/s, and x16 reaches 1,024 Gb/s, enough to clear 800G. So an 800G card has two ways past the choke point, and each has a price. Pick the host link before the optics, because the slot, not the cable, decides which card can run at line rate.
